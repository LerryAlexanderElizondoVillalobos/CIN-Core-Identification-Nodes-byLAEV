# Professional, Technical, and Educational Position on Design

**Scope:** Design, architecture, research, education, and systems development

## 1. Statement

As a designer and researcher, I maintain that a need must be capable of being understood, studied, and communicated independently of the solution proposed to address it.

A need does not belong to the designer who identifies it, the institution that studies it, the company that develops a response, or the technology used to implement that response. It exists independently of all of them. Likewise, understanding a problem should not depend on whether someone accepts a particular brand, methodology, architecture, platform, or proposal.

My position begins with a distinction that I consider fundamental to professional practice: **the need, the capability required to address it, and the solution developed are related realities, but they are not equivalent**. Confusing them can limit research, condition education, and cause a particular solution to take the conceptual place of the problem it was intended to address.

I do not consider it sufficient to design something that works. I also consider it part of my professional responsibility to explain what need motivated its development, what function it is intended to perform, under what conditions it can be considered appropriate, and what other solutions might fulfill that same function.

## 2. Conceptual Independence Between Need and Solution

When a need is presented exclusively through the name, image, or technology of a solution, there is a risk that the public will begin to associate the problem with that particular implementation.

At that point, the discussion may stop focusing on the need and instead become dependent on acceptance or rejection of the solution, its author, its owner, its brand, or its technical decisions.

A person may recognize that a problem exists while disagreeing with a particular solution. That distinction must remain possible to express without disagreement with an implementation becoming rejection of the need itself.

For this reason, I consider it necessary to maintain a clear conceptual distinction:

- **Need or problem:** The human, social, institutional, spatial, or technical condition that requires attention.
- **Functional capability:** What must be possible in order to respond to the need.
- **Requirement:** A verifiable condition that makes it possible to evaluate whether a capability is present and functions appropriately.
- **Mechanism:** The procedure, resource, or component that enables a function to be performed.
- **Architecture:** The organization of capabilities, components, relationships, and rules.
- **Implementation:** The concrete realization of an architecture through technologies, materials, processes, institutions, or systems.
- **Representation:** The name, language, image, interface, or form through which a solution is communicated.

These levels are related, but none should conceptually replace the others. An implementation can change without the need disappearing. A technology can become obsolete without the function ceasing to be necessary. A brand can disappear without the knowledge accumulated about the problem losing its value.

## 3. The Risk of Representation Displacing the Problem

The name and representation of a solution are legitimate resources for communicating, organizing, and identifying work. The problem is not naming a proposal or creating a visual identity for a project. The problem arises when those elements become the mandatory route through which the need must be understood.

In architecture, for example, the need for habitation does not belong to a particular building. In communication, the need to exchange information does not belong to a platform. In social organization, the need to coordinate responsibilities does not belong to a specific institution. In documentary preservation, the need to preserve and verify events does not belong to a database, a blockchain, or any particular technology.

These examples reveal the same issue: **a solution may represent a response to a need, but it must not claim ownership of that need's meaning**.

For this reason, when presenting research or a project, I consider it important to explain the problem before introducing the name of the solution. Those listening should have the opportunity to understand the need, evaluate its importance, and discuss its conditions without first having to accept the proposal being presented.

## 4. My Educational Position as a Professional and Designer

In design education, I believe we should not train professionals merely to produce solutions. We should train professionals who can recognize, investigate, describe, and evaluate the needs that make design necessary.

Teaching a solution without teaching how to understand the problem that gave rise to it can create dependence on existing methods, tools, or models. By contrast, teaching people to identify a need, formulate its conditions, and evaluate different responses allows knowledge to continue developing even as technologies and circumstances change.

Therefore, when I explain a project, I consider it important to teach at least the following:

1. **How to recognize a need:** Observe real conditions, distinguish symptoms from causes, and avoid assuming that the first interpretation of a problem is necessarily correct.
2. **How to formulate the problem:** Describe it precisely, establish its scope, and identify aspects that are not yet understood.
3. **How to distinguish facts, interpretations, and proposals:** State what has been observed, what is inferred, and what is being presented as a hypothesis or design decision.
4. **How to identify necessary capabilities:** Determine which functions would need to exist to address the problem before choosing a technology or a particular form.
5. **How to establish evaluation criteria:** Define what an appropriate response means and how its performance will be verified.
6. **How to compare alternatives:** Study more than one possible solution without confusing the designer's personal preference with an objective need.
7. **How to recognize limitations and consequences:** Explain what the proposal does not solve, what dependencies it introduces, and whom it may affect.
8. **How to transmit knowledge:** Document the reasoning clearly enough that other people can review it, challenge it, continue it, or propose different solutions.

My educational intention is not to teach others to reproduce my solutions. It is to contribute to their ability to develop independent judgment when understanding needs and building well-founded responses.

## 5. Design as a Process of Research and Reasoning

I understand design as an activity that integrates observation, analysis, formulation, organization, experimentation, evaluation, and communication.

The material or technological result is one part of the process, not the whole of it. Before building, it is necessary to understand what is being addressed. During development, it is necessary to verify that the decisions being made continue to respond to that need. After implementation, the results must be evaluated and the differences between what was expected and what was achieved must be acknowledged.

From this perspective, the design process should maintain conceptual traceability among:

- the identified need;
- the evidence and observations supporting its formulation;
- the capabilities considered necessary;
- the derived requirements;
- the architectural decisions;
- the selected mechanisms;
- the implementation developed;
- the observed results;
- the limitations and outstanding questions.

This relationship makes it possible to review a solution without having to accept all of its author's decisions. It also makes it possible to preserve useful knowledge when an implementation is modified or replaced.

## 6. Technical Neutrality and Evaluation of Alternatives

I do not consider it appropriate to present a technology as necessary merely because it was used in a solution. Technology selection must respond to the identified functions, conditions, and constraints.

A need for preservation, verification, coordination, or information exchange does not by itself determine that a blockchain, centralized database, distributed network, physical register, or any other mechanism must be used. Each alternative must be evaluated according to its purpose, properties, costs, limitations, and consequences.

The neutrality I propose does not mean that all alternatives are equivalent or that designers should avoid making decisions. It means that decisions must be justified by explicit criteria rather than by the promotion of a technology, a brand, or a personal preference.

When one alternative is more appropriate, it should be possible to explain why. When there is insufficient evidence to decide, that uncertainty should be acknowledged rather than concealed behind absolute claims.

## 7. Knowledge Independent of Its Author and Implementation

I believe that the knowledge produced during a design process must be able to outlast the person who developed it and the specific solution in which it was realized.

For that reason, professional documentation should describe the problem, foundations, criteria, functions, decisions, and limitations clearly enough for other people to understand them without depending on a private explanation from the author.

This also means accepting that a solution may be challenged, modified, replaced, or discarded. If an implementation ceases to be appropriate, the need that gave rise to it must not disappear from the research for that reason.

Knowledge must remain open to review. The continuity of research cannot depend on everyone adopting the same solution.

## 8. The Designer's Responsibility

I understand that professional responsibility does not end when an object, building, interface, system, or technical proposal is delivered.

There is also an intellectual and educational responsibility: to make the reasoning behind the result understandable, distinguish what has been demonstrated from what has been assumed, acknowledge limitations, and avoid presenting a solution in a way that suggests there are no other ways to address the need.

This responsibility requires the designer not to confuse authorship of a proposal with conceptual ownership of the need. Identifying, researching, or working on a problem does not make that problem the property of the person studying it.

My work should be evaluated on its arguments, decisions, and results. It should not require other people to adopt my name, my project, or my approach in order to recognize the importance of a need.

## 9. Applying This Position to Research and Development Projects

This statement establishes a general professional position. It is not the name of a product, platform, or particular technological architecture.

I include it in the CIN repository because I believe this initiative must also be examined according to the criteria set out here. However, the repository and its architecture are a specific implementation within a broader field of needs. They do not define those needs by themselves or exhaust the possible responses to them.

Accordingly, CIN documentation should seek to distinguish between:

- the human, social, administrative, or technical needs being investigated;
- the functional capabilities considered necessary;
- the principles and requirements guiding development;
- the proposed architecture for organizing those capabilities;
- the selected components and mechanisms;
- the concrete implementation and its actual development status.

This separation allows the project to be evaluated, compared, and improved without making its name or technology a condition for understanding the problem. It also allows other people to study the needs described and propose different responses.

## 10. Criteria for Communicating and Teaching a Proposal

As a practical guide for my presentations, documents, and educational activities, I establish the following criteria:

- Present the need and its context first; then present the proposed response.
- Explain the function before describing the component that implements it.
- Clearly distinguish facts, hypotheses, objectives, and design decisions.
- Do not use a project's name as a substitute for explaining the problem.
- Avoid presenting a technology as indispensable when it is only one implementation alternative.
- Explain the criteria used to select a solution.
- Present reasonable alternatives and explain why one of them is chosen.
- State limitations, dependencies, risks, and unresolved matters.
- Make it possible for knowledge to be reviewed and used without requiring allegiance to a brand or its author.
- Evaluate results according to how well they address the defined need and requirements, not according to the appearance or recognition of the proposal.

## 11. Principle of Conceptual Independence Between Need and Solution

To express this position in a concise form, I use the following statement:

> **The understanding of a need must remain independent of the solution proposed to address it.**

This principle establishes an orientation for research, design, education, and communication. It does not claim that needs can be described without context or that solutions should lack authorship or representation. It states that a need must be capable of being understood and discussed without acceptance of a particular implementation being a prerequisite.

The solution must respond to the need; the need must not become subordinate to the solution.

## 12. Final Statement

I do not want the world to have to know my solution in order to understand the problem I am trying to solve.

I want someone to be able to study the need, recognize its importance, question my interpretation, and propose another response without having to adopt the name, technology, or vision of the person who developed an initial implementation.

As a designer, I do not consider my responsibility to consist solely of building responses. It also consists of making the questions that made those responses necessary understandable and transmitting knowledge in a way that allows others to continue it.

We should not design in such a way that needs end up depending on our solutions. We should develop solutions that respond to needs whose understanding can remain independent of us.
